Case study · Healthcare research · Manuscript in preparation

Encouraging Web-Based Advance Directives at Hospital Admission: A Stepped-Wedge Trial

A pragmatic randomized trial across 16,020 hospital admissions that tested whether a low-touch nudge at registration could get more patients' care wishes into the medical record.

My roleStudy design, research methods, study implementation, and data collection.
SettingA large urban academic medical center, 700+ beds
DesignThree-group stepped-wedge pragmatic randomized trial, reported to CONSORT guidelines
Worked withPalliative care research center, health system innovation center, hospital admissions operations
16,020adult admissions in the trial
3admission sites, randomly sequenced
8 weeksfollow-up for each patient
8 monthstrial length, Feb to Oct 2019

The problem

Advance directives are widely promoted as a way to make end-of-life care match what patients want, yet there is no consistent evidence they improve care. Three barriers keep recurring. Few people complete one (even among Americans worried about end-of-life care, only about 27% have one). Completed directives are often not available to clinicians at the point of care. And many contain too little detail to guide a real decision.

A web platform that elicits values and detailed preferences, stores them securely, and moves them into the electronic record could address all three. But little was known about how to raise documentation of directives at the point of care.

Why hospital admission

Hospitals are required to ask every admitted patient whether they have an advance directive, so registration representatives already ask the question. That makes admission a natural, low-cost moment to point patients to a better tool. The design fit the existing registration workflow instead of adding a new one, so the intervention would be cheap to scale if it worked.

Eligible patients were adults, excluding maternity, admitted through the emergency department, the scheduled admissions unit, or pre-admission testing. These were the most common entry points, and each already had a registration step. Because the platform was in English, non-English-speaking patients were excluded.

The design

A stepped-wedge design suits a live admissions process, because every site eventually receives the intervention and each site serves as its own comparison. All three sites began with usual care, then switched to the intervention one at a time, in a randomly assigned order.

A 3-month baseline, then a new site switched every month, with at least 3 months of exposure at every site.

The intervention. A standardized, low-touch workflow in which registration representatives encouraged patients to complete a detailed directive on the web platform during admission. The platform had been developed earlier and tested with user-centered design methods.

The outcome. A new high-quality directive in the electronic record within 8 weeks. I held the bar high on purpose: a directive counted only if it went beyond naming a surrogate and stated at least one care preference.

What we found

Most patients did not have a usable directive, and the nudge did not change that.

Share of the 16,020 admissions

All admissions
100%
Already had a high-quality directive at admission
8.6%
Completed a new one within 8 weeks
4.7%

The intervention was not associated with completing a new directive. Who completed one mattered far more than which group they were in. In the draft analysis, patients 65 and older had about two and a half times the odds of a new directive, and patients with a cancer diagnosis and those who used intensive care also had higher odds. Black patients had substantially lower odds than white patients, a disparity that a one-size-fits-all nudge would not close.

What it means

  • A low-touch technology nudge is not enough. Health systems that want better directive documentation may need more resource-intensive approaches.
  • Technology has limits in advance care planning. The result tempers the assumption that a better website alone will change behavior.
  • Equity needs its own strategy. The gaps by age and race point to interventions matched to populations ready to engage.
  • A null result is still a finding. It tells other hospitals not to build a program around a registration-desk nudge without more support.

How it connects to earlier work

This trial builds on an earlier pilot randomized trial I co-authored, published in the American Journal of Hospice and Palliative Medicine. The pilot enrolled 91 patients with gastrointestinal and lung cancers and compared a web-based directive that sends plans to the electronic record against a paper form.

Differences were not statistically significant in the pilot. Satisfaction and acceptability were high in both groups.

The pilot suggested promise. The larger pragmatic trial tested whether that promise holds in a real admissions workflow at scale. Together they show the value of testing a digital tool in steps, from a small controlled pilot to real-world conditions.

Case study · Consumer app research

Travel Revamp Prototype Validation

Seven moderated sessions that validated a mobile MVP, moved cross-device sync from a someday feature to a premium hook, and reversed a low-priority call on the packing list.

My roleSole researcher. Brief, screener, testing protocol, moderation, synthesis, and readout.
TimelineAbout two weeks in field and synthesis
MethodsHuman-moderated prototype sessions, counterbalanced design, triangulation with two earlier studies
Worked withProduct stakeholders, design team
7moderated 45-minute sessions
2travel modes tested per participant
7 of 7participants saw the value of consolidation immediately
30 to 35%of travel feature users already pay for a subscription

The challenge

People planning trips were stitching together weather apps, flight trackers, calendars, notes apps, and spreadsheets. Earlier foundational research had already named that as the problem, and the team bet on a single mobile travel hub to consolidate it. The prototype was the first serious attempt to deliver on the bet, and it had to be tested before MVP scope locked.

The stakes were real: feature decisions were about to be fixed, the surface already served paying users, and the team had invested heavily in this direction. My study had to answer one question above all. Does this consolidate the experience the way users need, or does it only look like it does?

The research questions

01

Intent

Does the landing surface communicate its purpose without onboarding?

02

Time logic

Do users understand the time-based grouping of trips, from two weeks out to day-of?

03

Parity

Does feature parity hold across driving and flying modes?

04

Blockers

Where does the experience break, and what is the single biggest adoption blocker?

A method judgment call

The original brief proposed AI-moderated sessions. I changed it to human-moderated. Unmoderated sessions would have captured task completion and surface reactions. They would have missed three things a structured task list cannot probe: a cross-device pattern that emerged on its own, a willingness-to-pay signal that surfaced in a side conversation, and the anxious tone around flight warnings.

I also had every participant try both driving and flying modes, with the order counterbalanced to control for comparison bias. The cost was longer sessions. The cross-mode signal is what made the readout work.

Seven participants, first mode tested

DFFFDFD
D · driving first (3)F · flying first (4)

The split was 3 to 4, honest rather than pristine. Two further participants did not show up, and I wrapped at seven instead of recruiting replacements once theme saturation held.

How I synthesized

I kept a two-tab tracker, raw observations and a theme summary, and updated it after every session. I defined status thresholds upfront: strong, emerging, mixed, critical, and outlier. To avoid overclaiming from a small sample, I triangulated directional signals against two earlier studies, including a 198-person device differentiation survey.

Finding 1: consolidation lands

Everyone saw it at once. Participants named the tools this would replace: a flight tracker, a weather app, documents, spreadsheets, and pen and paper.

"This would replace the separate flight tracker app I use today."Frequent flyer participant

Finding 2: cross-device parity is a premium hook

Three of seven described planning on desktop and executing on mobile without being asked, and the earlier 198-person study corroborated it. One participant tied it directly to willingness to pay.

"Cross-device access is something I would pay for in a premium subscription."Mixed-mode traveler

What changed because of this research

  • The mobile MVP feature decisions held. The weather-driven packing list, blanket flight reminders, and mid-route weather were all validated, so the team did not need to reopen earlier decisions.
  • Cross-device sync moved from a later release to an MVP-adjacent premium hook. The willingness-to-pay signal, backed by the earlier study, turned "someday" into a monetization lever.
  • The packing list reversed from low impact to must auto-populate. Foundational research had rated it low priority, but every participant responded positively when it was tied to a real forecast. The spec is being rewritten to generate from weather and planned activities.
  • Findings seeded a desktop web strategy workshop through a one-page pre-read, and I identified three follow-on studies.

Reading the moment

The MVP slipped while my study was in field. The readout I had planned was a launch-gate decision document. After the slip, the same data had to do a different job: not "should we ship this?" but "here is what we learned and what it changes about MVP and web." I rebuilt the framing of the readout without rerunning the analysis. Same insights, different story arc. Matching the deliverable to the strategic moment, not to the original brief, is a craft skill I keep developing.

What I would test next: the desktop web prototype once it exists, the driving navigation hazard indicator that did not get a clean read this round, and where exactly people drop off between the two-week and day-of views.
