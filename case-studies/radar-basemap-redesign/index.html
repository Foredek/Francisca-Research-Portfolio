Radar Basemap Redesign: Three Rounds, One Pivot

A solo, three-round research program that caught a failing design direction before launch and moved a new basemap into production across three map modes.

My roleSole researcher. Program design, fieldwork, synthesis, and stakeholder readout.
TimelineAbout six weeks, April to May 2026
MethodsModerated concept testing, unmoderated A/B preference tests, adjective selection, open-ended coding

The challenge

Radar is the client's most-used feature, and the basemap is the screen people see every time they open it. Design wanted to modernize the palette against competitors and align it with a brand refresh.

Stakeholders first scoped a single unmoderated preference test. I pushed back. A one-shot vote on something this visible to this many people was not enough, so I rescoped the work into a three-round program. There were two ways to fail: ship a modern basemap that erodes daily engagement on the flagship feature, or freeze the current design while the brand moved on around it. Either would be visible to tens of millions of users.

The research strategy

I gave each round a different job and made each one feed the next. If I had used the same method three times, or skipped a round, the basemap either would not have shipped or would have shipped wrong.

Round 1 · Qualitative

Explore the design space

Seven one-on-one sessions of 45 minutes. I wrote the guide, moderated, and synthesized. The question was exploratory, so the method was qualitative.

Round 2 · Quantitative

Pressure-test the first direction

Unmoderated A/B test, 100 participants, four side-by-side comparisons. The question was whether the design regressed anything.

Round 3 · Quantitative

Validate the fix at scale

Unmoderated test, 102 participants, three modes side by side. The question was whether it was ready to ship.

The call I made. The obvious third round was a single-mode preference test. I refused that scope. Single-mode tests would have hidden the cross-mode polarization I had already seen hints of in round 2. I built a side-by-side, three-mode structure even though it meant longer sessions, and that structure surfaced the road color problems and gave design one decision frame instead of three.

Round 1: the direction landed, the symbols did not

Users called the map comprehensive, calm, less cluttered, and modern, and rated it above the other weather apps they use. But confidence stalled between 3 and 4 out of 5 for every participant. I traced the cap to ambiguous symbols such as toggles, the compass, and storm path numbers, not to the visual direction.

I also caught two specific bugs. "Light" and "very light" rain were both green, and the color key disappeared when users tapped the map. Small problems, large impact. I handed round 2 a clear brief: the aesthetic holds, so now prove the execution keeps contrast and label clarity at scale.

What participants compared in round 2

I did not stop at the headline. I read every open-ended answer across all four comparisons, and the same three words kept coming back, even in the comparisons the new direction won.

"Blurry, washed out, dismal. The muted colors made the storm look less serious than it is."Participant, round 2 light mode

The first "modern" direction was washed out, not premium, and on a radar map a muted storm reads as a less serious storm. I told design to stop and iterate. I did not scope round 3 until the palette had been reworked.

Round 3: proof the refined direction was ready

Round 3 tested the iterated palette in three modes side by side, counterbalanced. I added 1 to 5 scales for premium feel, clarity, and modernity, plus open-ended reasoning. The scales were my call. I needed diagnostic data, not just a vote.

Two findings that came from reading across rounds

The pivot. Each round had its own result, but the findings that drove the ship decision lived in the seams between rounds, where only the researcher who ran all three could see them. Reading the verbatims in round 2 exposed a regression the percentages hid.

The driver. Color contrast drove preference. Density, labels, and features did not move the needle. The diagnostic word-association layer I added in round 3 showed which words attached to which palette.

Impact

  • A failing direction was caught before it shipped. Without round 2 and a close read of the open-ends, the first direction would have reached millions of users and visibly regressed the light mode and article experiences.
  • A new basemap went into production in all three modes. Light was bundled in after the iteration brief I wrote from round 3.
  • Polarizing colors stayed out. Neither the yellow roads nor the orange roads carried into the new palette.
  • Leadership could move fast on a high-visibility change. Three rounds of triangulated data, delivered in about six weeks, were the de-risking.

What I took from it

  • The verbatim is the signal. The headline numbers in round 2 looked fine. The comments did not.
  • Method follows the question, not the calendar. A faster single-mode test would have hidden the pattern that mattered.
  • Program design is the lever. A single round would have failed this launch.

What I would do next: pair the shipped basemap with engagement data to confirm stated preference held up in real use, stress-test the palette under live severe weather overlays, and run an accessibility pass for color-vision deficiency, which preference testing did not cover.
