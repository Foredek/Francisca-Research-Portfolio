Read Every Open-Ended Response Before You Trust the Percentages

A preference test split two wins and two losses, and the headline numbers looked fine. The open-ended comments said the new design had a problem. Here is how I read them.

The result that looked fine

I ran an unmoderated A/B preference test with 100 participants on a redesign of a high-traffic map screen. Each person compared the current design with the new one in four side-by-side comparisons. The new design won two comparisons and the current design won two. A quick read of that is "mixed signal, split verdict," and a team under deadline could reasonably ship on it.

What the comments said

I read every open-ended answer across all four comparisons, not a sample. The same three words kept coming back, and they appeared even in the comparisons the new design won: blurry, washed out, dismal. One participant put it this way:

"Blurry, washed out, dismal. The muted colors made the storm look less serious than it is."

That comment is about more than taste. In a product where color carries meaning, a muted palette changes what the screen says. The percentages told me which option won. The verbatims told me the winning option had a flaw that would cost trust.

Why percentages alone mislead

  • A preference share says which option won, not why.
  • Two wins can hide a shared flaw. People can prefer the new design and still dislike how it looks.
  • Splits are often a sign that the comparison itself is not clean, so the reasons matter more than the margin.

A repeatable way to read open-ended responses

  1. Read all of them before you code anything. Sampling hides the repeated word you did not know to look for.
  2. Read by comparison and by outcome. Read the comments from the options that won as carefully as the ones that lost.
  3. Count recurring words, not only themes. Three people using the same unusual word is a stronger signal than a theme you named yourself.
  4. Attach one verbatim to each finding and check it against the source export.
  5. Tie each finding to a decision. If a finding does not change what the team does, say so and move on.

Where AI helps, and where it needs a check

AI can cluster several hundred comments in minutes, which is a real gain when you have a second round waiting. The risk is the same as with any summary: a paraphrase gets presented as a quote. I ask the tool to return exact quotes, then run a quote fidelity check against the raw export before anything goes in a readout. I wrote about that check in Two AI Quality Checks Every Moderated Study Should Run Before the Readout.

What happened next

I told the design team to stop and iterate instead of moving on. I did not scope the next round until the palette had been reworked. The next round tested the refined palette in three map modes side by side, and it gave leadership what they needed to ship. The full program is in the Radar basemap redesign case study.

Takeaways for researchers

  • Do not report a split verdict until you have read why people split.
  • Read every open-ended answer, including the ones from the option that won.
  • Whether you code by hand or with AI, check every quote against the source before it reaches a slide.
