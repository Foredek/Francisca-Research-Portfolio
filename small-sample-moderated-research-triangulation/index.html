Seven Sessions Is Enough If You Triangulate

How I got a defensible readout from seven moderated sessions: thresholds set up front, a two-tab tracker, counterbalancing, and a check against a larger study.

The setup

I validated a mobile travel prototype with seven moderated 45-minute sessions. Every participant tried two travel modes, driving and flying, and I counterbalanced which one came first. The split was three driving-first and four flying-first, honest rather than perfect. Two other participants did not show up. I stopped at seven instead of recruiting replacements, because the themes had stopped changing.

Set your thresholds before session one

Small samples invite overclaiming, so I decided how I would label evidence before I saw any. I used five status labels for every finding: strong, emerging, mixed, critical, and outlier. Deciding the labels in advance meant I could not quietly upgrade a finding because I liked it.

Keep a two-tab tracker

One tab holds raw observations from each session. The other holds a theme summary. I updated both after every session, so by the last session I was confirming patterns instead of discovering them under deadline pressure.

Report counts, not adjectives

  • Seven of seven participants saw the value of consolidating their travel tools immediately.
  • Three of seven described planning on desktop and running the trip on mobile without being asked.

"Many users" invites a stakeholder to picture a bigger number than you have. "Three of seven" does not.

Triangulate before you claim

Three of seven is a direction, not a result. I checked it against a 198-person device differentiation survey, which corroborated the cross-device pattern. That combination is what made it safe to recommend moving cross-device sync up as a premium feature. I also compared the findings with earlier foundational research. That check reversed one call: a packing list the earlier work had rated low priority turned out to matter once it was tied to a real forecast.

When the plan changes under you

The product launch slipped while my study was in the field. I had planned a launch-gate readout. After the slip, the same data had to answer a different question: what did we learn, and what does it change about the product and the web roadmap. I rebuilt the framing without rerunning the analysis. Same insights, different story arc. Match the deliverable to the decision the team now faces, not to the original brief.

Takeaways for researchers

  • Set evidence labels before fieldwork.
  • Write counts, and name the denominator.
  • Check small-sample patterns against a larger or earlier source before you recommend a roadmap change.
  • Reframe the readout when the roadmap moves.
